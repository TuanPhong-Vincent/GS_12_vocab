LESSON 1 – PAST SIMPLE VS. PAST CONTINUOUS

Past simple vs. Past continuous: forms and core uses
  • We use the past simple to describe a completed action at a specific time in the past, or main chronological events in a life story. e.g. Steve Jobs dropped out of Reed College in 1972.
  • We use the past continuous to describe an action in progress at a specific time in the past, or to set the historical background/setting of a narrative. e.g. At 9 p.m. yesterday, Nam was reading the wartime diary of Dang Thuy Tram.
Tense / Structure Grammatical Form Core Usage & Context Example (Unit Context)
Past Simple S + V-ed / V2
S + didn't + V-inf		 Completed action; historical milestone; sequence of events Dr. Dang Thuy Tram volunteered to join the army at the age of 24.
Past Continuous S + was / were + V-ing
S + wasn't / weren't + V-ing		 Action in progress at a specific past point; background scene She was treating wounded soldiers in a remote field clinic.
Interrupted Action Past Cont. + WHEN + Past Simple
or WHILE + Past Cont., Past Simple		 A shorter action interrupts or occurs in the middle of a longer action The surgeon was operating on a patient when the power suddenly went out.
Parallel Actions Past Cont. + WHILE + Past Cont. Two continuous actions taking place simultaneously in the past While grandfather was fighting at the front, grandmother was managing the family farm.
⚠ WATCH OUT!
  • We do not use the past continuous with stative verbs (verbs describing states, thoughts, senses, or emotions: know, believe, understand, realize, remember, love, want, belong).
    • Correct: 'She knew her duty was dangerous.' (NOT: She was knowing...)
  • When describing a succession of quick consecutive actions in a biography, use the Past Simple for every verb in the sequence.
    • Example: 'He left Apple in 1985, founded NeXT, and purchased Pixar.'
Time clauses with When, While, and As
  • We frequently use time conjunctions (when, while, as) to link two clauses describing past actions.
  • As a rule of thumb, while and as introduce a longer background action (past continuous), whereas when often introduces a short or interrupting event (past simple).
Conjunction Clause Pattern Pedagogical Function Authentic Example
While / As While + S + was/were + V-ing, S + V-ed Introduces background duration before the main event While Steve Jobs was auditing calligraphy classes, he developed a love for typography.
When S + was/were + V-ing + when + S + V-ed Points to the specific moment an unexpected event happened The team was finalizing the animation software when the computer system crashed.
While (Simultaneous) While + Past Cont., Past Cont. Highlights two continuous activities in parallel While Nam was reading the diary, his brother was writing the historical summary.
When (Immediate) When + Past Simple, Past Simple Action in the 'when' clause triggers the next immediate action When the soldier found the diary, he decided not to burn it.
⚠ WATCH OUT!
  • If the time clause introduced by While or When stands at the beginning of the sentence, place a comma (,) after it. If it comes at the end, usually no comma is required.
    • While Dr. Tram was tending to the wounded, an explosion rocked the hill.
    • An explosion rocked the hill while Dr. Tram was tending to the wounded.
Spelling rules & verb inflection: -ed and -ing
  • Pay close attention to consonant doubling and silent 'e' deletion when forming past tense and present participles:
Base Form Rule Past Simple (-ed) Past Continuous (-ing) Examples
Consonant + single vowel + consonant (stressed) Double consonant + -ed Double consonant + -ing drop → dropped, dropping
occur → occurred, occurring
Ending in silent -e Add -d Drop -e + -ing devote → devoted, devoting
operate → operated, operating
Irregular Past Verbs V2 form (special change) Regular -ing addition write → wrote, writing
fly → flew, flying

LESSON 2 – ARTICLES (REVIEW & EXTENSION)

Indefinite articles: A and An
  • We use the indefinite articles a and an before singular, countable nouns when the listener does not know exactly which person or thing we are referring to (first mention). e.g. I want to visit a cultural exhibition.
  • We also use a / an when stating someone's job, religion, or nationality: She is an anthropologist; he is a craftsman.
Article Phonetic Rule Usage Note Example (Unit Context)
a Before words beginning with a consonant sound Includes vowels pronounced with /j/ or /w/ sounds He bought a handcrafted souvenir from a European vendor.
an Before words beginning with a vowel sound Includes silent 'h' or acronyms starting with vowel sounds It was an honor to meet an elderly artisan from an ethnic community.
⚠ WATCH OUT!
  • Always choose a or an based on the spoken sound, not the written letter!
    • a university (begins with consonant sound /j/), a one-day festival (/wʌn/).
    • an hour (silent 'h' /aʊər/), an honest person, an MP3 player (/em/).
Definite article: The (Rules and Special Extensions)
  • We use the definite article the before singular or plural nouns when both the speaker and listener know which specific entity is meant.
Category Specific Rule Example (Unit Context)
Unique Entities Things of which there is only one in the world or universe The sun shines brightly over the equator.
Second Mention Nouns that have already been introduced previously in the discourse A craftsman made a bronze drum. The drum was displayed in the cultural museum.
Musical Instruments When referring to playing or learning a musical instrument Linh is learning to play the Dan Bau; Peter plays the guitar.
Superlatives & Ordinals With superlative adjectives and ordinal numbers This is the most vibrant festival; Charles Lindbergh was the first person to fly solo.
Plural / Compound Countries Countries with 'Kingdom', 'States', 'Republic', or plural nouns the UK, the US, the Philippines, the Netherlands.
Oceans, Seas, Mountain Ranges Names of oceans, seas, rivers, canals, deserts, and mountain chains The Pacific, the Atlantic, the Alps, the Mekong River.
⚠ WATCH OUT!
  • Do NOT use 'the' with single mountains or single lakes: Mount Everest (NOT the Mount Everest), Lake Baikal (NOT the Lake Baikal).
  • Do NOT use 'the' with most individual country names, continents, or cities: Viet Nam, Japan, Asia, Paris (NOT the Viet Nam).
Zero article (Ø) / No article
  • We do not use an article before plural countable nouns or uncountable nouns when speaking about them in a broad, general sense.
Classification Grammar Rule Example
General Plural Nouns Plural countable nouns referring to a whole class or group Ø Tigers are endangered animals; Ø museums preserve cultural heritage.
General Uncountable Nouns Abstract concepts, materials, or substances in general We cherish Ø peace, Ø mutual respect, and Ø cultural diversity.
Meals, Sports & Languages Routine meals, games/sports, and languages (without the word 'language') They enjoyed Ø lunch together, played Ø badminton, and spoke Ø English.
⚠ WATCH OUT!
  • Contrast general vs. specific use:
    • General: 'I love Ø traditional cuisine.'
    • Specific: 'I love the traditional cuisine of Hue.' (Defined by the phrase 'of Hue').
  • Notice the language difference:
    • 'She speaks Ø Japanese.' BUT: 'She speaks the Japanese language.'

LESSON 3 – VERBS WITH PREPOSITIONS & RELATIVE CLAUSES

Verbs with prepositions: collocations and patterns
  • In English, many verbs are followed by specific prepositions. These combinations form fixed verb collocations that cannot be changed. e.g. Many households depend on solar energy.
  • The preposition is always followed by a noun phrase, pronoun, or gerund (V-ing).
Preposition Common Verb Collocations Context Meaning Example (Unit Context)
for apologize for, blame for, search for, care for, prepare for Expresses purpose, cause, reason, or responsibility The chemical plant apologized for polluting the local river ecosystem.
to belong to, contribute to, adapt to, object to, listen to Expresses direction, recipient, or consequence Excessive plastic packaging contributes to catastrophic marine waste.
on rely on, depend on, concentrate on, insist on, base on Expresses reliance, focus, or foundation Successful green living depends on grassroots community initiatives.
in succeed in, participate in, believe in, result in, invest in Expresses involvement, success, or outcome The school community succeeded in reducing single-use plastics by 80%.
from protect from, prevent from, recover from, suffer from Expresses separation, origin, or defense Planting urban trees protects citizens from hazardous air pollution.
about worry about, care about, complain about, think about Expresses mental focus, concern, or emotion Environmentalists worry about rising global temperatures and ice melts.
⚠ WATCH OUT!
  • Preposition + Gerund Rule: A verb coming directly after a preposition MUST take the -ing form (gerund). Never use a bare infinitive or to-infinitive!
    • Correct: 'They succeeded in conserving freshwater.' (NOT: succeeded in conserve)
  • Beware of 'to' as a preposition: In verbs like contribute to, look forward to, object to, 'to' is a preposition, so it is followed by V-ing:
    • 'Everyone can contribute to protecting the planet.'
Relative clauses referring to a whole sentence (with 'which')
  • We can use a non-defining relative clause introduced by which to comment on or describe the result of an entire preceding clause, not just the single noun before it.
  • This structure creates elegant, cohesive academic writing by showing cause, effect, or evaluation in a single flowing sentence.
Structure / Pattern Communicative Purpose Example (Unit Context)
[Clause], which + verb... States a direct result or outcome of the preceding fact The city council installed solar lighting, which saved thousands of kilowatt-hours.
[Clause], which is + adjective... Provides the writer's judgment, attitude, or comment Many families have started composting kitchen waste, which is highly commendable.
[Clause], which means that... Explains the logical consequence or implication Students bring their own water bottles, which means that no plastic trash is created.
⚠ WATCH OUT!
  • You MUST always use a comma (,) before which when it refers to the entire sentence!
    • The students cleaned the park, which impressed local residents.
  • You can NEVER use 'that' or 'what' to replace 'which' in this sentence-commenting structure!
    • Wrong: The students cleaned the park, that impressed everyone.
    • Wrong: The students cleaned the park, what impressed everyone.
